A lyric that is half a second late is worse than no lyric at all. It pulls your eye away from the song. It makes a good track feel broken.

So when people tell us the words are out of time, we take it seriously. Almost always, the app is doing exactly what it was told. The timing is off for one of three reasons.

## The file was timed against a different master

Timed lyrics are written by ear, against one recording. Change the intro, cut a verse for radio, add a cold open on the album version, and every timestamp after that moment is wrong.

The words are still the right words. They just arrive at the wrong time. This is the most common cause, and it is nobody's fault.

## The player reports a different name

Lyrics are matched on title and artist. A feature credit, a remaster suffix, a live version with the same title, or a translated artist name can all send the lookup somewhere else.

Lyrica shows the exact title and artist it read from Windows in the status panel. If that does not match what you are hearing, the lookup is chasing the wrong record.

## The track has no timed lyrics at all

Plenty of songs have plain text lyrics but no timestamps. Plain words can still be shown, but they cannot follow a song line by line. That is a different problem from drift, and the status panel says which one you have.

## The fix is one control

If the words are consistently early or late, use the nudge control. `-1s` and `+1s` shift the whole lyric by a second. Most records need one tap, some need two. Then it sits correctly for the rest of the song.

The offset is stored per song and cleared when the next track starts. That last part matters. A global offset is a trap: you fix one album and quietly ruin everything else.

We also split lines into words using the real line duration, not a fixed delay. On a slow ballad, words breathe. On a fast verse, they keep up. If a record has word-level timestamps, we use those instead.

## Three checks before you nudge

Before you touch the offset, confirm three things. First, the status panel's title and artist match the track you are hearing. A remaster suffix or a featured credit can send the lookup to a different recording, and no amount of nudging will fix a different song.

Second, check whether the drift is constant or growing. If every line is exactly one second late, a nudge is the right tool. If the first line is close and the last line is four seconds late, the lyric file was timed against a different edit; a single offset will fix one end and ruin the other.

Third, play the track from the beginning once. Some players report a position that includes a previous track's tail, or resume from a cached position after a pause. A clean start removes that variable before you blame the file.

## Why the offset is per song

A global offset is a tempting shortcut. It is also a trap. One album might be authored against a slightly slower master, while the next is exact. If you shift everything to fix the first, you break the second, and the next day you cannot remember which setting caused it.

Per-song offsets are deliberately local. They live with the track, they clear when the next track starts, and they never silently change the timing of something you did not touch. There is no global offset to forget about later, and no hidden state that follows you to the next record.

The same logic applies to word timing. We use the line's real duration to distribute words, so a slow line breathes and a fast line keeps up. When the provider supplies word-level timestamps, we use those instead. When it does not, we do not invent them.

## What we will not do

We will not guess. If a record has no timing data, we would rather show plain words than invent a rhythm. A wrong animation is worse than a still one.

If a song keeps drifting after a nudge, the file is probably matched to a different master. Send us the title and the version you are playing. We keep a list, and it helps more people than you would think.
