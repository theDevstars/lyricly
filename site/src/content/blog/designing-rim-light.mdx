The first version of Rim Light was too much. We hung a neon frame around the display and called it ambience. It looked like a gaming keyboard had swallowed a monitor.

The second version was too little. A thin strip of color, tasteful, invisible from a metre away. No point.

The version we shipped sits in the middle, and the middle took longer than either extreme.

## Start with the edge, not the blur

A screen is a light source. Light from a screen lands on the surface behind it. That is the whole idea.

So we draw the color at the border first, at full strength, then let the glow fall off. If you turn the glow down, you still get a crisp edge. If you turn it up, the color spills further into the room without turning into fog.

Thickness and glow are separate controls on purpose. A thick, tight rim reads as hardware. A thin, wide bloom reads as atmosphere. Both are useful, and neither is a preset.

## Two colors, with a bias

Album art rarely has one useful color. It has a dominant tone and a supporting one. Forcing a single color flattens the artwork into a mood ring.

Two colors let us follow that structure. Color A carries 60% of the gradient, color B 40%. The split is fixed, because a slider for it produced a lot of combinations that looked like mistakes.

When Override album color is off, the palette comes from the artwork. When it is on, you choose. The rest of the settings stay put either way.

## Motion should be optional, and slow

Breathe is a five-second cycle. Drift is slower still. Neither is a visualizer. They should register in your peripheral vision, not demand attention every beat.

We added Still because some people want the color and nothing else. That is a valid taste, not an accessibility checkbox. It is also the right setting for recording, where a moving gradient can fight a video encoder.

## The boring constraints

Rim Light has to survive the taskbar. It has to work on a second monitor with a different scale. It has to leave the wallpaper alone. It has to cost almost nothing in frame time.

Most of the work was not the glow. It was making a window that sits behind everything, draws once per frame, and never steals a click. The settings panel is the fun part. The rest is plumbing.

## Settings that did not ship

We built a beat-reactive mode. It looked spectacular in a demo and exhausting after three songs. A screen edge that flashes on every kick turns the room into a strobe, and it fights any video that is already cut to the music. It is not in the app.

We also built a per-corner color mode. Four colors, four corners, a gradient around the frame. It was fun for about a minute and then looked like a gaming peripheral. The two-color version reads as light from the artwork; four colors read as a light show.

A “match the album cover exactly” sampler exists internally, but it produced muddy colors on covers with a lot of grey. The shipped sampler biases toward the most saturated region instead. That is a small lie about the artwork, but a much better result on a wall.

## What we learned

Ambient light is a restraint problem. Every time the effect got more impressive, it got less usable. The settings we kept are the ones you can leave alone.

If you try one thing, try Still with a thick rim and low glow. It looks like the screen is made of colored glass. That is the version we kept coming back to.
